# Decoded: Reading a Riskometer Label Like a Professional

*A "Decoded" piece from Abundance Financial Services · Atin Kumar Agrawal, ARN-251838*

Every mutual fund factsheet carries a little dial graphic — the riskometer — pointing somewhere between "Low" and "Very High." Most investors glance at it once, register a vague impression, and move on. It deserves a closer read than that, for two reasons most people don't know.

## The six levels, and what they're actually measuring

SEBI's riskometer runs across six bands: Low, Low to Moderate, Moderate, Moderately High, High, and Very High. It's calculated using a defined formula that weighs a fund's market risk, credit risk (for debt holdings), liquidity risk, and volatility — not a subjective label the AMC chooses, a standardised calculation every fund house has to apply the same way. That standardisation is genuinely useful: a "Moderately High" equity fund and a "Moderately High" hybrid fund were scored on the same scale, even though the underlying holdings look completely different.

## The part nobody tells you: it can change without you doing anything

This is the one that catches people off guard. The riskometer isn't set once at launch and left alone — AMCs are required to recompute and, if needed, update it **monthly**. A fund's risk label can shift from "Moderately High" to "High" purely because market volatility increased, or because the portfolio's credit mix drifted, even if you never touched your investment and the fund manager never changed strategy. If you check your factsheet one year and the dial has moved, that's not necessarily a red flag about the fund — it's the label doing its job and reflecting current conditions.

## The misconception that causes real damage

"Moderate risk" does not mean "safe." It means moderate *relative to the other five bands on this specific scale* — and even a "Low to Moderate" debt fund can lose money in the wrong credit event. The riskometer is a relative ranking tool, not a guarantee, and definitely not a substitute for actually reading what the fund holds.

## How to actually use it

The riskometer is most useful compared against one thing: **your own risk capacity**, which is a completely separate label the industry now also requires — a Risk-o-meter for you, based on your own product-suitability questionnaire responses. A fund's risk label matching your own risk profile's label is a genuinely useful sanity check before you invest. A fund's risk label alone, read in isolation, tells you almost nothing about whether it belongs in your specific portfolio.

Next time you check a factsheet, don't just glance at where the needle points. Check *when it last moved*, and ask whether your own comfort with volatility still matches it.
